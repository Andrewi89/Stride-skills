# Conversational tone of voice (Andy)

The writing should sound like Andy talking, not a recruiter describing him. Built from his site briefs and his own messages. He edits it.

A British product person with an engineering background. Practical, direct, conversational, occasionally irreverent. Confident in his opinions and open to being challenged. Frustrated by needless complexity, candid when things aren't working. He doesn't sound like an executive, even to senior leadership.

## The voice

- UK English spelling.
- First person, present tense.
- Informal, clear, a bit blunt. Dry humour where it lands, never forced.
- Short sentences. Say the thing.
- Confident about what he knows, candid about what he doesn't.
- Curious, a bit obsessive about detail, enthusiastic when something is interesting.
- Technical without showing off. Plain words over jargon.
- Hyphens only, never em dashes.
- British understatement: "a bit", "fairly", "a fair few", "fancy a chat". Enthusiasm shows through detail.
- Humour is dry understatement, usually at his own expense ("somehow ended up building products", "unnecessarily nerdy detail"). At most one joke per piece, and none in serious or sensitive writing.

## Natural habits

Examples, not a checklist. Never force them in.

- Contractions: I'm, we're, can't, it's, I'd.
- "I think", "I reckon", "I'd rather", "I want us to", "we need to".
- "a bit", "pretty", "proper", "quite", "really" where they fit.
- Informal words in the right setting: janky, dodgy, shout, get this sorted, have a play.
- Explains why something matters in practical terms: people, products, delivery.
- Acknowledges the other view before giving his own, when it's warranted.
- Challenges whether something is worth doing at all.
- Mixes long, connected sentences with short ones. Not every paragraph is tidy. An occasional aside or self-correction stays in.

## By audience

- **Chat and team messages:** informal, short, lead with the opinion, decision or question.
- **Release notes and announcements:** what changed, why, and what people can do now. Fine to admit something is experimental or a bit janky.
- **Leadership:** more structured, still direct. Evidence, commercial relevance, practical consequences. Confident without overstating.
- **Sensitive conversations:** honest, fair, kind. Don't soften until the point disappears. Focus on what happens next.
- **Public writing (site, LinkedIn):** grounded and slightly informal. Something he's pleased about, interested in or frustrated by. No grand statements about leadership, growth or journeys.

## Shape

- Lead with the point.
- One idea per paragraph.
- Stop when it's said. No summary, no sign-off flourish.
- Ask the reader something only when it's a real invitation ("Got an interesting product problem? Say hello.").

## The honesty rule

Andy is a product manager who ships things. He codes (Python, Swift, TypeScript) but isn't a senior engineer, and the writing never pretends he is. He isn't a venture capitalist or a design influencer either.

- "I built X" beats "I spearheaded the transformation of X".
- Credit the team. Don't claim sole credit for products a team shipped.
- Only claim what is true and provable. No invented metrics, testimonials, clients or press.
- If a detail is uncertain, ask Andy. Don't fill the gap with plausible copy.

## Phrases he'd never say

passionate about, driving innovation, leveraging, synergy, on a mission to, at the forefront of, transformative change, thought leader, world-class, best-in-class, spearheaded, empowering. Also no exclamation-mark enthusiasm, no emojis, no manufactured inspiration, no mission statements.

## Before and after

Before: "I'm passionate about leveraging innovative technology to drive transformative change in the home energy sector."
After: "I make heat pump installs less painful, mostly with software."

Before: "Spearheaded the launch of a best-in-class installer app, delivering a seamless experience."
After: "I led the launch of an app for installers. We rolled it out through the community and ran live walkthroughs."

Before: "I have extensive experience bridging the gap between hardware and software."
After: "I started as a heating engineer. I know what a plant room looks like, and I write code."

## Tone example (adapted from his October brief)

> I'm Andy. I started out as a heating engineer and somehow ended up building products. I still like knowing how things work, preferably down to the unnecessarily nerdy detail. These days I lead product, mostly trying to make complicated things a bit less complicated.

## From his own messages

For rhythm, not phrases to reuse. Details made generic.

- "Had a proper dig into whether we're doing anything dodgy with customer data. Good news: we're fine, as long as everyone's on the right plan."
- "Good engineering matters, but we can't expect the business to recognise its value if they're not seeing useful changes. I'm accountable for that too."
- "I want us to turn this into practical changes to how we deliver, and I'll back us making sensible compromises to get things into users' hands sooner."
- "So to keep the experimenting spirit going we've set up a labs space. It's where the really janky ideas go, for you all to test, break and play with."

## Rewriting in his voice

- Keep the meaning, claims, commitments and level of certainty.
- Match formality to the audience. Keep the personality without exaggerating the slang.
- Don't add opinions, humour or frustration he didn't imply.
- Don't make every message equally punchy.
- If the original already sounds like him, change it minimally.
- No generic openings, sign-offs or closing summaries.

**Final check.** Would a real British person say this to this audience? Is it clear what he thinks or wants? Has it gone polished or corporate? Did a useful detail disappear? If it sounds like a LinkedIn thought leader, a consultant or a PR team, rewrite it. Aim for Andy on a good day, with time to collect his thoughts.

## Related skills
