# Marketing for tech products

Work in this order. Later steps are wasted if earlier ones are wrong. Sources and the one idea taken from each are in `references/sources.md`. Read it when you need the detail.

## 1. Positioning (Dunford)

Fill in five lines before anything else:

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what do customers do today if this product didn't exist? (A rival, a spreadsheet, nothing.)
2. **Unique attributes:** what do we have that those alternatives lack?
3. **Value:** what do those attributes let customers do or avoid?
4. **Best-fit customers:** who cares most about that value?
5. **Category:** what market frame makes the value obvious?

If you can't fill a line with something specific, do more research before writing any copy.

Prefer an existing category people already understand, sharpened with a subcategory. Creating a new category is slow and expensive.

## 2. Beachhead segment (Moore)

Pick one narrow group you can win completely: a specific job, a specific type of customer, a whole product that solves their problem end to end. Early adopters can hide that the mainstream wants something different. Expand to the neighbouring segment once the first is won.

## 3. The promise and the words

Turn positioning into messaging first: one sentence a customer would repeat, then 2 to 4 supporting points, each with proof. Then one promise, one big idea. Write headlines and body with `ogilvy-copy`, then clean with `anti-slop-writing`. Tech buyers distrust adjectives. Lead with proof: a demo, a number, a customer's real result, a plain explanation of how it works.

## 4. Get recognised (Sharp)

Recognition has two jobs.

- **Mental availability:** people think of you when the need arises. Link the product to specific buying situations ("when a team has to estimate a project") and repeat it.
- **Physical availability:** the product is easy to find, try and buy. Remove steps between interest and use.

Use a few distinctive, consistent assets (name, colour, shape, phrase, sound) and keep them for years. Aim to reach more of the right people, not only to please current ones.

## 5. Choose channels by testing (Weinberg)

1. List every channel that could plausibly reach your segment (search, content, communities, partnerships, events, PR, sales, email, ads, integrations, referrals).
2. Rank them by likely reach and cost.
3. Run a small, cheap test on the top 3, each with a clear success number set beforehand.
4. Double down on the one that works. Drop the rest for now.

## 6. Balance brand and activation (Binet and Field)

Long-term brand work builds future demand. Short-term activation harvests today's. Treat about 60:40 brand to activation as a consumer starting point, less for B2B, and adjust from results. All activation and no brand stalls once the easy buyers are gone.

## 7. Launch by tier

Match the launch to the impact. A big change for many customers gets the full treatment (announcement, page, email, demo, briefing for anyone who sells). A small improvement gets a release note. Over-launching small things teaches people to ignore you.

## 8. Measure

Pick one number per stage: reach, trial or sign-up, activation, paid, retained. Check it weekly. Kill what doesn't move it.

## Questions to ask first

- Who is the one customer type we serve first?
- What do they use today, and why is it not good enough?
- What is the single promise, in one sentence they would repeat?
- Which channel can we test this month for under a set budget?
- What would make us stop?

For network products (marketplaces, communities), start with Chen's idea: get one small network working before scaling.
