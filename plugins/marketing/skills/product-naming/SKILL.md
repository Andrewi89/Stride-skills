# Product Naming

A structured approach to brand naming drawn from professional naming methodology. The goal
is not to find a comfortable name - it's to find the *right* name, which will likely feel
uncomfortable at first.

## Core principles

Before generating anything, internalise these:

**Describe nothing.** Descriptive names (Infoseek, TripActions, ProChip) are forgettable,
crowded, and limiting. The name should start a story, not make a statement.

**Comfort is a bad signal.** If the team immediately likes a name, it's probably too safe.
Polarisation - some people love it, some hate it - is a sign of energy and distinctiveness.
Sonos was rejected. Azure was called "a dumb idea." Both became enormous brands.

**Invent an experience, not a word.** The best names evoke behaviour, feeling, or movement.
Windsurf (flow, speed, dynamism). Swiffer (quick, joyful action). BlackBerry (reliable,
tangible, memorable).

**Compounds multiply associations.** Two-part names like PowerBook, Facebook, Windsurf
produce more associations than their components alone (1 + 1 = 3). Don't dismiss them for
being "long".

**Invented words are cheaper to build than real ones.** They signal novelty, face less
trademark congestion, and generate more attention precisely because they're unexpected.
Pentium, Sonos, Dasani, Vercel - all invented or substantially altered words.

**Sound carries meaning.** Letters evoke distinct qualities:
- V: alive, vibrant, energetic (Vercel, Viagra, Corvette)
- B: reliable, solid, trustworthy (BlackBerry, Bold)
- Z: noisy, attention-grabbing (Azure, Sonos)
- X: fast, crisp, innovative
- S: fluid, slightly noisy, technical
- Hard stops (K, T): decisive, confident

## Step 1 - Run the Diamond Framework

Before generating names, help the user get clarity on what winning looks like. Draw out (or
ask them to work through) four questions:

```
         WIN
        /    \
  HAVE TO    NEED TO
        \    /
        SAY TO
```

- **WIN** - How do you define winning? Not "grow revenue" - what does breakthrough look
  like? ("The most innovative, fastest-moving company in the category." / "People ask for
  us by name.")
- **HAVE TO WIN** - What assets do you already possess? Founders underestimate this.
  Tech, audience, trust, distribution, category knowledge.
- **NEED TO WIN** - What's missing? Talent, distribution, perception, credibility in a
  new segment.
- **SAY TO WIN** - What must the name communicate? Push beyond the obvious. Not just
  "fast" but "flow". Not just "efficient" but "joyful".

Then translate the SAY TO answer into **behaviour and experience**: how should the
marketplace behave towards this brand, and how should the brand behave in the marketplace?
This is the creative direction.

---

## Step 2 - Landscape the competition

Before inventing names, map what already exists. Note:
- Existing brand names in the category and adjacent categories
- The language and descriptors competitors use
- What naming conventions dominate (portmanteaus? compound words? abstract coined terms?
  scientific-sounding names?)

The goal is to identify white space - where the name can be distinctive. If every
competitor uses tech-Latin (Synaptiq, Neurex, Computia), a single vivid English compound
stands out.

---

## Step 3 - Generate in quantity

Generate at least 50-100 raw candidates before evaluating anything. Organise into
directions, not just names. Directions might include:

- **Coined words with familiar components** (Vercel = ver- + -cel; Pentium = pent + -ium)
- **Compounds from unexpected domains** (Windsurf, BlackBerry, Swiffer - all from outside
  the category)
- **Metaphor from movement/flow/nature** if the product is about speed or ease
- **Sound-led exploration** - names built around specific letters that match the desired
  character (e.g. V-initial names for an energy/performance product)
- **Palindromes or reversible structures** for products about balance or universality
  (Sonos, Navan, Civic)

Quantity breeds quality. Don't evaluate during generation. Write "speculate, don't
evaluate" at the top of the list if it helps.

Useful forcing constraint: generate names as if the product is from a completely different
category (outdoor equipment, kitchen appliances, a toy). Many winning names came from
teams working on a disguised brief.

---

## Step 4 - Shortlist and evaluate

Once you have a substantial list, apply these filters in order:

**Distinctiveness** - Does it stand out from the competitive landscape? Would someone say
"they're not like the other guys"?

**Sound character** - Does the sound match the desired experience? Say it aloud. Does it
feel fast, reliable, alive, smooth, noisy? Check the first letter against the sound
symbolism guide above.

**Processing fluency** - Is it easy for the brain to process? Familiar components assembled
in an unfamiliar way is the sweet spot. Complexity is a liability.

**Compound multiplier** - If it's a compound, are the associations from both parts
pointing in useful directions?

**Flexibility** - Can the brand grow beyond its current scope without the name becoming
a liability? (TripActions became a problem when they expanded into finance. Navan didn't.)

**Polarisation test** - Present the shortlist to a small group and look for names that
split the room. Not the name everyone finds fine - the name half the room loves and half
the room questions.

**Linguistic/cultural check** - For any name going to market: check for unintended
meanings in relevant languages, cultural associations, and homophone problems. This catches
things that sound like disasters in Australian English, have sexual connotations in another
market, or rhyme with something unfortunate.

---

## Step 5 - Test candidate names externally

The best informal test: tell someone "our competitor just launched and they're called
[NAME]." Watch their reaction. Are they curious? Intrigued? Do they lean in? That response
- not "does this fit our product" - is what you want.

Do not ask people "do you like this name?" or "does this fit our product?" That question
always selects descriptive names. Ask instead: "what do you think this company does?" and
"how does it make you feel about the product?"

---

## Step 6 - Build prototypes

Before presenting internally, mock up the name in context:
- On a product screenshot or UI
- In a hypothetical press headline ("Introducing [NAME]...")
- On a T-shirt or sticker
- In a short one-liner: "[NAME] is the [category] that [key behaviour]."

This helps internal stakeholders see it as a real brand rather than reacting to an abstract
word.

---

## Handling internal resistance

If the client or stakeholder team resists a strong name:

- Reframe from "does this fit us" to "does this work in the marketplace"
- Point to polarisation as a positive signal, not a problem
- Show competitors' names alongside it - distinctiveness becomes obvious in context
- Remind them: design, messaging, and products all change. The name stays.
- Use the prototype materials to make the future concrete rather than abstract

---

## Common failure modes to avoid

- **Settling at 200 names.** You need 1,000+ directions before shortlisting. 200 feels
  like a lot; it isn't.
- **Large brainstorms.** Four or more people in a room produces consensus, not boldness.
  Use pairs or individuals with different briefs.
- **Evaluating too early.** Generate first, speculate second, evaluate third.
- **The .com trap.** Domain availability is an area code. Get the right name first. A
  $20-30k domain purchase or a .ai suffix beats a mediocre name with a clean .com.
- **Describing the product.** If you can read what the product does from the name, it's
  probably wrong.
- **Comfort as validation.** "Everyone likes it" means it's forgettable.
